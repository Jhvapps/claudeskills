# UK Crowdfunding: Legal, Tax & Deal Structures (2026)

Compiled from live research, August 2026, reflecting the 2026/27 UK tax year (post Autumn Budget 2025, effective 6 April 2026) and the new FCA Public Offer Platform regime (effective 19 January 2026). **Always get HMRC Advance Assurance and a direct platform fee quote before marketing a raise — figures below are for planning, not final numbers.**

## 1. SEIS and EIS — the core UK equity-raise sweeteners

Only apply to genuine new ordinary share issuance. Irrelevant to loan notes, RBF, or community shares.

| | SEIS | EIS (standard) | EIS (Knowledge Intensive) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investor income tax relief | **50%** | **30%** | 30% |
| Max investor relief per tax year | £200,000 | £1,000,000 | £2,000,000 |
| CGT on gain (held 3yrs+) | Exempt | Exempt | Exempt |
| Max company age | ≤3 years trading | ≤7 years from first sale | ≤10 years from first sale |
| Company lifetime raise cap | £250,000 | **£24,000,000** (from £12m, eff. 6 Apr 2026) | **£40,000,000** (from £20m) |
| Company raise cap per 12mo | (same as lifetime) | **£10,000,000** (from £5m) | **£20,000,000** (from £10m) |
| Gross assets test | ≤£350,000 | ≤£30m before / ≤£35m after (from £15m/£16m) | same |
| Employee test | <25 FTE | <250 FTE | <500 FTE |
| Min holding period | 3 years | 3 years | 3 years |

**Note**: the EIS limits above roughly doubled from the Autumn Budget 2025, effective 6 April 2026 — confirm against the live gov.uk EIS guidance before relying on them, given how recent the change is. SEIS limits are unchanged since April 2023.

**Risk-to-capital condition** (in force since 2018, applies to both): the company must have genuine long-term growth objectives, and there must be significant risk the investor could lose more than they gain. This is the mechanism that catches asset-heavy businesses — see hospitality note below.

**Connected persons rule**: an investor (with associates — spouse, parents, children, business partners) can't hold or be entitled to acquire more than **30%** of the company and still claim relief. Employee-directors generally can't claim on their own investment (narrow founder exceptions exist).

## 2. Excluded activities — read this before assuming any venue qualifies

A trade is disqualified if a **substantial part (HMRC's working benchmark: >20%)** of it consists of: dealing in land/shares/financial instruments, banking/insurance/money-lending, leasing/letting assets, legal/accountancy services, property development, farming, forestry, **operating/managing hotels** (or comparable overnight-accommodation businesses) **if the company has an estate/interest in or occupies the premises**, **operating/managing nursing/residential care homes** (same premises caveat), energy generation, and services provided to another business carrying on an excluded activity under common control.

Bars and restaurants with **no accommodation** are not excluded — ordinary retail/wholesale F&B is a qualifying trade.

### Hospitality deep-dive (directly relevant to a multi-venue group)

- **A hotel is excluded** if the company owns/occupies the premises being run as a hotel — because accommodation is a core, non-ancillary part of the business, not a small side activity under the ~20% threshold. **A venue marketed and trading as a hotel + pub is a high-risk SEIS/EIS candidate for this reason** and should not be promised tax relief without HMRC Advance Assurance in hand first.
- **Rooms bolted onto a pub can be treated as ancillary** (non-disqualifying) only if accommodation revenue is genuinely small (well under ~20%) and the business markets itself as "a pub," not "a place to stay" — this is a facts-and-degree test HMRC applies by looking at marketing materials, not just the accounts split.
- **Renting out event/function space** is similarly excluded "leasing/letting" if it becomes a substantial share of revenue.
- **The risk-to-capital condition independently penalises property-heavy businesses** — even a nominally qualifying F&B trade can fail if the company owns significant freehold/leasehold value that "de-risks" the investment in HMRC's eyes.
- **Structuring point**: keep the trading company (bar/restaurant operations) separate from any property-owning entity, and avoid growing primarily through freehold acquisition inside the SEIS/EIS vehicle. When buying an existing venue, HMRC expects the company to be acquiring the physical space only — not the existing trading name/goodwill/staff — or it risks being treated as continuing an existing trade rather than starting a new qualifying one.

**Always get HMRC Advance Assurance before marketing a SEIS/EIS raise** — platforms and serious investors won't participate without it, and it's the only reliable way to de-risk the "substantial part" and risk-to-capital judgement calls.

## 3. Deal mechanics

### Nominee vs direct shareholding
Most UK platforms (Crowdcube especially) use a **nominee structure**: the platform's nominee entity is the single legal shareholder of record; thousands of crowd investors are beneficial owners of a slice of that holding, retaining economic rights (dividends, SEIS/EIS eligibility) and voting via the nominee polling beneficial holders. This keeps your actual company register to one line instead of hundreds/thousands of individual shareholders. **Direct ownership** (investors registered individually) is available on some platforms as a company-level choice — simpler legally for investors, much heavier cap-table admin for you.

### Valuation
No formal methodology at this stage — set by founder proposal tested against investor demand. Common reference approaches: comparables (recent similar-stage raises), Scorecard/Bill Payne method, milestone-based, VC method (back-calculated from target exit/IRR). Treat any "typical" £ figure as rough market colour, not a rule — wide sector/geography variance.

### Advance Subscription Agreements (ASAs) — the UK-standard convertible instrument
UK platforms use **ASAs, not US-style SAFEs**, specifically because a correctly structured ASA can preserve SEIS/EIS eligibility.
- Investor pays now for a contractual right to shares later (typically at the next priced round).
- **Discount**: typically 15–25% (most commonly ~20%) off the future round price.
- **Valuation cap** protects against a valuation spike before conversion.
- **Long-stop date**: HMRC wants this **≤6 months** from the ASA date to grant Advance Assurance comfortably.
- Must be "clean" for SEIS/EIS: no interest, subscription cannot be refunded, cannot be varied/cancelled/assigned, no debt-like investor protections.

### Convertible loan notes (CLNs) — NOT SEIS/EIS compatible
HMRC treats CLNs as debt until conversion — shares issued on conversion of a loan **do not qualify** for SEIS/EIS relief. If tax relief is part of the pitch, the instrument must be an ASA or straight equity, never a CLN. CLNs remain legitimate for bridge funding where SEIS/EIS isn't the objective (e.g. a hotel-type venue that wouldn't qualify anyway).

Typical CLN terms where used: 15–25% discount (~20% common), 5–8% interest (often rolled into shares at conversion rather than paid cash), 18–24 month maturity, negotiated valuation cap. Risk: if no further round happens before maturity, the repayment obligation can crystallise as a real cash liability.

### Investor rights (standard platform template)
Pre-emption rights (first refusal on new issuance), information rights (at least annual updates), drag-along (majority can force minority to sell in an exit), tag-along (minority can join a majority sale on the same terms). Pre-emption typically needs to be explicitly subordinated to drag-along in drafting, or it can block an exit. Crowd investors under a nominee structure generally don't get board seats — reserved for lead/institutional investors.

### Platform fees (get a direct quote — these shift)
- Crowdcube: ~6–7% success fee + completion fee (~7.75–8.5% all-in); investor pays 2.49% + 5% carry on profit.
- Republic Europe (was Seedrs): ~6% + ~1–2% ongoing nominee admin fee (or a higher fixed-fee structure per some sources — confirm directly); investor pays ~2.5% + 5% carry.

## 4. FCA regulatory requirements

- **The platform, not the fundraising company, needs FCA authorisation** — running/operating an equity crowdfunding site is a regulated activity ("arranging deals in investments," often also "holding client money"), requiring a Part 4A permission. This is why raises go through an already-authorised platform rather than a DIY page.
- **Financial promotion rules**: any communication inviting investment in unlisted shares must be issued/approved by an FCA-authorised firm. Direct retail offers of these "non-mainstream pooled investments" are restricted to investors who self-certify as **High Net Worth** (income ≥£100k or net assets ≥£250k), **certified/restricted sophisticated**, or are receiving regulated advice.
- **New Public Offer Platform (POP) regime, live 19 January 2026**: offers **below £5m** are exempt from a prospectus; **£5m+** must go through an authorised POP. Most Coal & Co / Chef Pantry-scale raises are unaffected — only relevant at £5m+. This regime is very recent — re-check FCA policy statements before relying on it for a large raise.

## 5. Community Benefit Society (CBS) — legal detail

- Governed by the Co-operative and Community Benefit Societies Act 2014. Registered with the **FCA**, not Companies House.
- **One member, one vote** regardless of stake. Capital is **withdrawable, not transferable** (often locked for an initial 3+ years while reserves build). No capital growth — return is a capped interest rate (e.g. "base rate + 2%, or 5%, whichever is greater"), set at the minimum needed to attract capital.
- Can adopt a statutory **asset lock**: assets must be used for community benefit in perpetuity, even on wind-up.
- **CITR is a separate, narrower relief** than SEIS/EIS — 25% relief spread over 5 years, but only applies to investment into accredited **CDFIs**, not directly to a typical pub BenCom unless that BenCom is itself CDFI-accredited. Don't present CITR as a general community-shares relief.
- Typical single pub raise: £150k–£450k from 100–400 investors (community shares supply ~57% of total start-up capital on average, balance from grants/loans).

## 6. Alternative structures — quick terms reference

**Revenue-based finance**: 5–15% of monthly revenue until a repayment cap is hit (cap commonly 1.1x–2x, up to 3x cited elsewhere — negotiate case by case). No dilution, no SEIS/EIS eligibility (it's debt). UK providers fund up to £10m, approval in 24–72 hours for smaller facilities.

**Convertible loan notes** (where SEIS/EIS isn't the goal): 15–25% discount, 5–8% interest, 18–24 month maturity.

**SAFEs**: the US-market convertible instrument — not UK standard practice (UK uses ASAs instead, purpose-built for SEIS/EIS compatibility).

## 7. US context (secondary — for comparison only)

Reg CF cap: $5m/12 months (unchanged 2026). Non-accredited investors capped at $124,000 across all Reg CF offerings/12 months. No SEIS/EIS equivalent at the federal level.
