# Crowdfunding Case Studies — Hospitality, F&B, Consumer & Marketplace

Compiled from live research, August 2026. Real named examples with real figures. Some figures conflict across sources — flagged inline.

## BrewDog — "Equity for Punks" (the definitive case study, and its cautionary ending)

- **£75m raised from ~220,000 investors** across 5 UK rounds (EFP I-V, 2009-2021) plus separate BrewDog USA rounds. Average outlay ~£400-500/investor.
- EFP V (Oct 2017): £26.2m from 50,000+ investors — hit a £10m target in under 90 days, ran a full 12 months, "world's most successful equity crowdfunding raise" at the time.
- **Valuation trajectory**: ~£26m at first raise (2010) → TSG Consumer Partners (US PE) bought ~22% for £213m in 2017, implying ~£1bn valuation ("unicorn") → peaked at £2bn in 2021 → **sold to Tilray Brands (US cannabis firm) for £33m in 2026** after five years of losses (£37m loss in 2025).
- An early £230 share (2010) was reportedly worth £6,590 by 2017 — a 2,765% paper gain that evaporated for anyone who didn't cash out.

**Marketing tactics that worked**: framed investing as joining "the craft beer revolution," not a financial product. Lifetime perks tied to shareholding (20+ shares = 10% lifetime bar/shop discount). Zero paid advertising in early years — relied on PR stunts and press. Later rounds engineered visible fast-fill momentum to build FOMO for the next tranche.

**What went wrong — the structural lesson**: because TSG's 2017 institutional stake carried a liquidation preference (repaid first), the entire £33m Tilray sale was absorbed by institutional creditors/preference shareholders. **All ~220,000 retail investors — who put in £75m collectively — got £0.** Investors also learned of the sale from news reports, not from the company directly.

**The takeaway for a skill/knowledge base**: crowdfunding-as-marketing-and-loyalty-engine can be extraordinarily effective for growth — but if later institutional rounds stack preferences above the crowd, a "successful" business can still leave every retail investor with nothing at exit. **Deal structure and preference stacking matter as much as marketing execution.** Never let a lead/institutional investor's terms subordinate the crowd without understanding exactly what that means at exit.

## UK pub crowdfunding / community ownership (Plunkett Foundation model)

Sector-wide: **180,000+ people** have invested in Plunkett-supported community-owned businesses, raising **£50m** collectively; community pubs specifically via "More than a Pub" raised **£14m+**.

- **The Auctioneers Arms** (Caverswall, Staffordshire): £200k+ raised, 130+ investors, £250-£6,000 tickets.
- **The New Inn** (Norton Lindsey, Warwickshire): needed ~£450k total (freehold £312.5k + refurb + working capital).
- **The Ivy House** (Nunhead, London) — London's first co-operatively owned pub, first Asset of Community Value pub purchase under the Localism Act. Shares from £200, **371 member-shareholders**, blended with an Architectural Heritage Fund loan + Social Investment Business grant. ~12 months from threat-of-closure to reopening (Aug 2013), first profit reported for year to Feb 2017.

**Pattern**: typical single-pub raise £150k-£500k, 100-400 local investors, £200-£250 minimum tickets, campaigns run months not weeks, often blended with heritage/social investment grants rather than pure crowd equity.

## Marketplace / tech startup crowdfunding — closest comps for Chef Pantry

### JustPark — peer-to-peer parking marketplace (best direct marketplace comp)
- 2015: £3.7m in 34 days (370% of £1m target, then a UK record). ~2,900 investors, ~£1,200 average ticket.
- 2019: £1m in the first 54 minutes, £5.5m in 48 hours, £7m final from 5,200 investors.
- **£17m cumulative from 10,000+ investors** across rounds, used explicitly for international expansion.
- **Why it worked as a marketplace play**: every investor is a potential supply-side (driveway owner) or demand-side (driver) user — crowdfunding doubled as a distribution/awareness channel for a genuinely two-sided marketplace. **This is the exact mechanic transferable to a chef/venue marketplace**: investors on both sides of the network become liquidity.

### Freetrade — fintech investing app (best "crowd-as-marketing-engine" comp)
- 7 rounds total on Crowdcube. Later rounds hit £6-8m each in **under 6 hours**. One round drew investors from 63 countries.
- Valuation jumped £270m → £650m across rounds. **13,000 total crowd investors** — became Freetrade's *largest* shareholder bloc, bigger than VC backer Draper Esprit.
- **Lesson**: repeated, planned crowd rounds (not one-off) can be used deliberately as a recurring growth/PR moment and a way to convert users into evangelist-owners.

### E-Car Club — electric car-sharing marketplace (small raise, real exit)
- £100k from 63 investors (2013) for 20% equity. Later raised £500k from a Centrica-backed impact fund, then **acquired by Europcar in 2015** — Crowdcube's claimed first-ever successful crowdfunding exit, investors got a multiple return.
- **Lesson**: crowdfunding doesn't need huge numbers to work if the exit is clean — good counter-example to BrewDog's scale.

## Notable failures — cautionary tales

### Chilango "Burrito Bond" — mini-bond collapse
£2.16m raised (2014) via mini-bond (not equity), promising 8% annual interest over 4 years. Chilango entered administration; bondholders faced up to **99% losses** in restructuring (offered 10p-in-the-£1 recovery or a share swap). **Lesson**: mini-bonds carry minimal legal protection — a headline "biggest raise ever" is not a safety signal.

### Pact Coffee — campaign withdrawn mid-flight
Targeted £1m, pulled the campaign after reaching only **19% (<£200k) from 931 pledgers** — despite already having £5.6m from credible VC backers. **Lesson**: VC pedigree and brand awareness don't guarantee crowd appetite — retail investors respond to an ownership/belonging pitch, not a professional-investor pitch.

### Emoov — online estate agent marketplace collapse (closest marketplace-failure comp)
Raised £1.84m (2018) from 1,067 investors, above its £1m target — **then revised its own valuation from £104m down to £51.8m at the tail end of the same round**, giving investors just 4 days to decide whether to proceed at the new terms. Months later entered administration when promised merger funding never materialised. 32+ formal investor complaints filed with Crowdcube; other companies cited "the curse of Emoov" and withdrew their own raises afterward.

**Lesson — most relevant to a marketplace raise like Chef Pantry**: a mid-campaign valuation cut combined with time pressure to still commit is a major red flag pattern. Reputational damage from a collapse can spread to *other, unrelated* raises on the same platform — crowdfunding carries correlated trust risk across a sector, not just company-specific risk. Keep investor communication and valuation integrity airtight throughout the campaign, not just at launch.

### Other named UK crowdfunded failures (lighter detail)
- **East End Manufacturing**: £400k raised, folded Sept 2018.
- **Hen Restaurants**: £150k raised, closed within a year.
- **Pronto** (food delivery, Seedrs): £100k raised June, shut down by September — 3 months.
- **Property Partner** (real estate crowdfunding platform): not a single collapse but a slow multi-year wind-down, Trustpilot rating fell to 1.3 stars — an example of "slow bleed" failure vs sudden collapse.
